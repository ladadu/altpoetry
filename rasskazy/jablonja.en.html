The Apple Tree. [101.MO]

RU

On summer evenings, I love walking through my neighborhood, along the paths between the mushroom-like houses that have grown up, tall stern guardian trees, and startled, jumping squirrels. I love to walk the exact same route: sometimes climbing up familiar hills, sometimes following a sharp turn of the road to go down. The houses succeed one another, and they are all different: some are white, some are made of red brick. In each of them lies life, a different kind of life. In one of the houses, there are so many children that they are scattered like peas across the front porch. Next to the adjacent house is a whole garage of expensive, elite cars. And then there is a house where an elderly couple lives, who go out for a walk every evening, holding hands. And this massive house is empty and listed for sale.

Gardens live around the houses. They are just as different as the houses, and as the people who inhabit them. Here are the stern structures of fir trees, their needles cleaning the space around the house like brushes, saying: "Do not come near, noise and extra people are not welcome here." And this garden is all flowers and decorations; it laughs with the childish soul of its elderly owners, shouting: "Look, we are all sunbeams, we are a rainbow path, we are children's fairy tales, no matter how old we are!" But this garden over here is not a garden at all—it is more like a vegetable patch. Only the useful grows here: fruits, legumes, sunflowers, tomato sprouts. In this house, people have no time for silence or fairy tales; they need to survive, so they grow food. And the next house has no garden at all—just a hedge of tightly trimmed bushes. It is isolated from everything that might prevent its owner from spending his resource of time optimally and efficiently. Most likely, a businessman or a lawyer lives here.

But my way does not only lie among the houses. There is a place on my path that runs along the very edge of the highway. There is always the noise of car engines, the light of their headlights, and the high-speed spinning of wheels. My path runs between this highway and a piece of an old, abandoned garden. Every time, I pass by a tall, mighty tree whose branches lean almost over the highway. At first glance, this tree is no different from the nearby oak and chestnut... But I wait for autumn. This tree and I have a secret, a ritual, a relationship, and a history.

Once, passing by it at the end of August, I broke away from the smooth flow of my thoughts and looked up—at the arm-branches of this tree. They were stretched out to me, they were giving me something, offering and asking: "Take it, please, take it." These were apples. Very small "paradise" apples. The old apple tree bore them every year, just as her nature prescribed. But there were no longer any people to eat these apples, rejoice in them, make jam, or bake apple pies. Bees still pollinated her flowers, and birds pecked at the pulp of the apples. Her fruits fell to the ground, sprouted, but people would come and mow down the grass along with the sprouts of her seeds...

I look at her branch stretched out toward me; there are dozens of fruits on it. The tree is old, its strength is fading, and many of the fruits are stripped of immune defense, eaten by beetles, covered in cobwebs and signs of rot. But there are very healthy, yellow, smooth little apples. I pluck one, taste it—it is delicious, sweet, juicy. I look at the apple tree with gratitude; she whispers: "Take more." I pluck apple after apple; they are small, just one or two bites. I stuff my pockets with them; I will enjoy them on my way, I will bring them home and place them on a plate, I will remember you, apple tree. It is already getting dark, my pockets are bulging with the delicious harvest, it is time for me to go home. I look at the apple tree and see her peace and gratitude for the acceptance of the only thing she was once planted for.

I look at her with understanding and gratitude. I stroke her branches: "I will return to you tomorrow, little apple tree, and again next August. I will be glad of the treat you have prepared. You do not live in vain; your apples are still needed."
